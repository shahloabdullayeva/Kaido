# Breakdowns and road service
When a truck breaks down on the road, the job is to get it moving again safely, fast, and at a fair price.

## Overview
When a truck breaks down on the road, the job is to get it moving again safely, fast, and at a fair price. Every hour down means a late load, an unhappy customer and a driver not earning.

## The call flow

- **Driver calls in.** Get the basics first: unit number, exact location (mile marker, exit, GPS, or truck stop name), whether the truck is in a safe spot, and whether it's loaded (and if a reefer, what temperature the load needs).
- **Find out what's wrong.** Symptoms in the driver's words, dash lights, fault codes (SPN/FMI), when it started, and whether the truck still moves (derate, or dead).
- **Decide the fix.** Can the driver do a simple fix (a regen, reset, drain water from fuel)? Does it need a mobile mechanic on site, a tire truck, or a tow to a shop?
- **Find a vendor.** Nearby truck stops (TA Petro, Love's, Pilot Flying J), dealers, independent shops, mobile mechanics. Compare what they can do and how fast.
- **Get a quote and approve it.** Ask for parts and labor separately, the hourly labor rate, the call out (service call) fee, and mileage fees for mobile service. Approve in writing when possible, with a PO or authorization number.
- **Pay.** Often through the fuel card system: a **Comdata Express Code / Comchek**, an **EFS money code**, or a WEX check, or the vendor charges the card directly. This is where your fuel card knowledge already fits right in.
- **Close it out.** Get the final invoice, check it against the quote, and log the repair in the unit's record.

**Most common road calls:** tires (flats, blowouts), derates from emissions problems (DPF, DEF, NOx sensors, EGR), no start (batteries, starter), air leaks, lights, coolant leaks and overheating, broken belts, blown charge air hoses, trailer issues, and reefer units.

## How to read a repair invoice

- **Header:** vendor, date, unit number, VIN, mileage (odometer) and often engine hours.
- **Complaint, cause, correction:** what the driver said, what the mechanic found, what was done. A good invoice has all three.
- **Parts lines:** part number, description, quantity, price. Position matters for tires and brakes (LF, RRO and so on).
- **Labor:** hours times the rate. Shops charge from a book time for each job, not always the actual time.
- **Fees:** service call, mileage, shop supplies, environmental and disposal fees, taxes.

## Red flags

- Labor hours that don't match the job, or labor with no clear complaint and correction.
- Parts replaced that don't connect to the complaint (brake chambers on an overheating call).
- The same part replaced on the same unit a short time ago: could be a warranty claim instead.
- Tire lines without position, size, or new vs used. A grade like **Grade A** means used (an S prefix on a part number may or may not), and a used tire priced like a new one is worth questioning.
- Mileage or VIN that doesn't match the unit.
- Big jobs approved without a quote, or a final invoice far above the quote.
- Diagnostic fees charged more than once for the same problem.

**Warranty.** New trucks and many new parts are under warranty. Dealers can often do warranty repairs at no cost, so always check warranty before paying for engine, aftertreatment or transmission work on a newer truck.
