## Overview
Drivers live in the truck for weeks, so AC, heat and cab problems count as real repairs, especially in summer, when a dead AC in a sleeper can keep a driver from resting legally.

**How truck AC works.** It works like a home AC or fridge. The **compressor** (driven by an engine belt, switched on and off by a magnetic clutch) squeezes refrigerant gas and makes it hot. The hot gas goes to the **condenser**, a thin radiator in front of the engine radiator, where it cools and turns to liquid. It passes through a **receiver drier** (removes moisture) and an **expansion valve**, which drops the pressure so it turns ice cold. Inside the dashboard, the cold refrigerant flows through the **evaporator**, and the **blower motor** pushes cab air across it. Then it goes back to the compressor and the cycle repeats.

| Part | What it does | Common problems |
|---|---|---|
| AC compressor and clutch | Pumps the refrigerant | Clutch won't engage, compressor seizes, noise |
| Condenser | Releases heat from the refrigerant at the front of the truck | Punctured by road debris, clogged with bugs |
| Receiver drier (or accumulator) | Removes moisture from the refrigerant | Replaced whenever the system is opened |
| Expansion valve (or orifice tube) | Meters refrigerant into the evaporator | Sticks, blocked |
| Evaporator | The cold coil inside the dash | Leaks, clogged, bad smell |
| Blower motor and resistor | Blows air into the cab | Fan dead, only works on one speed |
| AC lines and hoses | Carry refrigerant | Leaks at fittings; the most common AC failure |
| Pressure switches | Protect the system | Faulty switch stops the compressor |
| Blend door actuators | Mix hot and cold air | Stuck on hot or cold |
| Refrigerant | Most trucks use **R-134a**; some newer ones use **R-1234yf** | Low refrigerant means there's a leak somewhere |

An **AC recharge** means vacuuming out the system and filling it with the correct amount of refrigerant. If the AC was low, there's a leak, so a recharge alone often doesn't last. Shops use UV dye to find it.

**Heat.** Cab heat comes from hot engine coolant through the **heater core**. Sleepers often have a separate **bunk heater** (Espar or Webasto), a small diesel burner that heats the cab while the engine is off.

**APU (auxiliary power unit).** Lets the driver run AC, heat and power while parked without idling the engine (idling wastes fuel and is illegal in some states). A **diesel APU** (Thermo King TriPac, Carrier ComfortPro) has its own small engine needing oil changes and filters. An **electric or battery APU** (Dometic, Bergstrom) runs off extra batteries charged while driving.

## Hub caps: two different things

- **Steer axle hub cap (oil hub cap):** the cap in the center of the front wheel with a clear window. It holds oil for the wheel bearings, and the window lets you check the level. A cracked or leaking one is a safety item, since the bearings can fail without oil.
- **Hub covers and lug nut covers:** chrome or plastic covers on the wheels that are mostly cosmetic. They're cheap, but they come up on invoices a lot.

**Mud flaps.** Rubber flaps behind the drive and trailer tires that stop spray and rocks from hitting the vehicles behind. Many states require them, and a missing one can be written up at a roadside inspection. The flap and its **hanger bracket** are separate parts and break often.

**Other body items you'll see on invoices:** mirrors (heated, motorized, often broken by other trucks), mirror glass, windshield (chips and cracks; a crack in the driver's view is a violation), wipers, headlight assemblies, bumper, hood (fiberglass, expensive), fenders, aero fairings and side skirts, door seals and handles, steps and grab handles, battery box covers, fuel tank straps, and the fire extinguisher and triangles.
