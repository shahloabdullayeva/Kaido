# The truck at a glance
What a semi is made of, the makes and models you will hear, and how to read a unit.

## Tractor and trailer
A "truck" in a fleet almost always means a **Class 8 tractor**: the part with the engine and the cab. It pulls a **trailer** that carries the freight. The two connect at the **fifth wheel** (on the tractor) and the **kingpin** (on the trailer), plus air lines and an electrical cord. The tractor and trailer are maintained separately and usually have separate unit numbers.

![Tractor (1) and trailer (2): 3 engine, 4 cab, 5 sleeper, 6 roof fairing, 7 fuel tanks, 8 fifth wheel, 9 cargo box, 10 landing gear, 11 trailer tandem](truck-diagram.png)

Class 8 means a gross vehicle weight rating over 33,000 lb. A loaded tractor-trailer may legally run at up to **80,000 lb** gross on US interstates without a special permit.

## Day cab and sleeper
- **Sleeper:** a bed and storage behind the seats. Used for long haul (OTR, over the road). Most fleets you will work with run sleepers.
- **Day cab:** no bed. Used for local and regional work where drivers go home every night.
- **APU (auxiliary power unit):** a small engine or battery pack on a sleeper that runs heat and AC while the main engine is off. Saves fuel and engine hours. Diesel APUs need their own oil changes and filters; electric ones need battery care.

## Reading a unit
- **Unit number:** the fleet's own number painted on the truck. Every call starts with it.
- **VIN:** 17 characters. Character 10 is the model year (L=2020, M=2021, N=2022, P=2023, R=2024, S=2025, T=2026). The VIN tells the dealer exactly what is built into the truck.
- **Odometer and engine hours:** service is due by miles, hours or months, whichever comes first. Idle-heavy trucks wear by hours more than miles.
- **Plate and registration (IRP/apportioned plates):** trucks crossing states carry apportioned plates. Expired registration is a roadside violation.

## The big systems
Every truck has the same systems. Each one has its own page in Learn:
1. Engine: makes the power.
2. Aftertreatment: cleans the exhaust. The most common reason a truck stops.
3. Fuel and DEF: what the engine burns and what the exhaust needs.
4. Cooling and AC: keeps the engine and the driver at the right temperature.
5. Air system and brakes: a compressor makes air, the air applies the brakes.
6. Tires and wheels.
7. Electrical: batteries, starting, charging, lights, computers.
8. Drivetrain: clutch, transmission, driveshaft, axles.
9. Steering and suspension.
10. Fifth wheel and coupling.
11. Trailer: brakes, lights, tires, doors, and on a reefer, the refrigeration unit.

## Axles and positions
A typical tractor is a **6x4**: six wheel positions (hub ends), four of them driven.

![Tire positions from above](tire-positions.svg)

- **Steer axle:** the front axle, 2 tires, about 12,000 lb.
- **Drive axles:** two rear axles together, called a **tandem**, 8 tires, up to 34,000 lb.
- **Trailer tandem:** 8 tires, up to 34,000 lb, usually on a **sliding** frame so the weight can be moved between axles.

Positions are named from the driver's seat: left = driver side, right = passenger side; inner and outer on duals. "Left rear drive, outside" or "LRO" is a typical way to say where a tire is.

## More detail

![Where the main parts sit on a tractor (side view, front on the left)](fs-parts-side-view.png)

![18 wheels: 2 steer, 8 drive, 8 trailer, seen from above](fs-18-wheels.png)

A semi (also called a tractor trailer or an 18 wheeler) is two vehicles hooked together: the **tractor**, which has the engine and cab, and the **trailer**, which carries the freight. They connect through the **fifth wheel**, a greasy horseshoe shaped plate on the back of the tractor, which locks onto a pin under the trailer called the **kingpin**. Fleets track tractors and trailers as separate units with separate repairs.

The brand matters because it tells you the engine family, which dealer can fix it, and which parts fit. Freightliner and Western Star share a lot of parts, and so do Peterbilt and Kenworth.

## Cab types

- **Sleeper:** has a bed behind the seats for long haul drivers who live in the truck for weeks. A raised roof sleeper is tall enough to stand in; a mid roof or flat top is lower.
- **Day cab:** no bed. Used for local and regional runs where the driver sleeps at home.
- **Conventional vs cab over:** almost every US truck is a conventional (engine under a long hood in front of the driver). Cab over trucks (flat front, engine under the cab) are standard in Europe but rare in the US.

## Axles

A normal highway tractor is a **6x4**: six wheel ends, four of them driven by the engine. That means one **steer axle** in front and two **drive axles** in back (together called a **tandem**). The trailer usually has two axles, also a tandem. Local day cabs are sometimes **4x2**, with just one drive axle.

Steer tires are single, one on each side. Drive and trailer axles run **duals**: two tires side by side on each end, an **inner** and an **outer**. That is where the 18 comes from: 2 + 8 + 8.

**Naming positions.** Left always means the driver's side. Fleets name each tire by side, axle and inner/outer, for example **LF** (left front steer), **RF** (right front steer), or **RRO** (right side, rear drive axle, outer tire). Some fleets number the positions instead. When a shop invoice says a tire was replaced, the position tells you which one, and you can check it against what the driver reported.

**Weight limits worth knowing.** The US federal max is 80,000 lb gross without a special permit: about 12,000 lb on the steer axle, 34,000 on the drive tandem and 34,000 on the trailer tandem.
