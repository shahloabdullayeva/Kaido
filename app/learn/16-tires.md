# Tires and wheels
Positions, sizes, tread rules, pressure, recaps and road service.

## Positions and types

![Steer, drive and trailer positions, inner and outer](tire-positions.svg)

| Position | Count | Tire type | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Steer | 2 | Steer (rib) tread | Most important for safety. New tires only on many fleets, no recaps |
| Drive | 8 | Drive (lug) tread | Traction. Recaps are common |
| Trailer | 8 | Trailer (free-rolling) tread | Recaps are common. Many curb and scrub damages |

**Duals:** two tires side by side. **Wide-base singles (super singles):** one wide tire instead of a dual pair; lighter and better mpg, but no "limp" if one fails.

## Reading a tire size
**295/75R22.5**: 295 mm wide, sidewall height is 75% of the width, radial, fits a 22.5-inch wheel. Other common sizes: 11R22.5, 11R24.5, 285/75R24.5, and wide-base 445/50R22.5.

**Load range** (G, H): how much weight it can carry at a given pressure. **DOT code** on the sidewall: the last four digits are the week and year it was made (2523 = week 25 of 2023).

## Tread depth (the legal minimums)
- Steer tires: **4/32 inch**.
- Drive and trailer tires: **2/32 inch**.

Fleets usually pull steer tires at 6/32 to 8/32 and send drive and trailer tires to recap before they go too low, so the casing is still good.

## Pressure
Most truck tires run around **100 to 110 psi** cold (check the tire maker's load chart). Underinflation is the number one killer of tires: it builds heat and ends in a blowout or a "zipper" sidewall failure. A tire run flat on a dual will often damage its partner too. **Tire pressure monitoring** and automatic inflation systems on trailers help.

## Recaps (retreads)
A worn tire's casing is inspected and new tread is bonded on. Costs about half of a new tire. A good casing can be recapped 2 or 3 times. Fleets track their casings; a "casing credit" is paid back for good ones.

## Wheels
- **Steel or aluminum.** Aluminum is lighter and costs more.
- **Hub-piloted** (most today) wheels center on the hub. Lug nuts are torqued to about **450 to 500 ft-lb** and **re-torqued** after 50 to 100 miles when a wheel has been off.
- **Loose wheel signs:** rust streaks from the lug nuts, shiny worn spots. A lost wheel is a serious accident risk.
- **Wheel seals and hubs:** oil-filled or grease-filled hubs. A leaking wheel seal throws oil on the brakes. A failed bearing can lead to a wheel-end fire.

## Alignment
Bad alignment eats steer tires in weeks. Signs: feathered or one-side wear on steer tires, the truck pulling to one side. Alignment checks belong in the PM schedule and after any front-end repair.

## Road service tire calls
1. Unit, position (for example "right rear drive, inside"), size from the sidewall.
2. Is the tire flat, blown, or just low? Is the partner tire damaged?
3. Is the wheel damaged?
4. Safe location? Shoulder, ramp, truck stop.
5. New or recap? Many fleets send recaps for drive and trailer, new for steer.

Road service tires cost more than shop tires, plus a service call fee and mileage. A driver who can limp to the next truck stop safely (on a dual, slowly, after inspection) can save money, but never on a steer tire.

## Tire brands you will hear
Michelin, Bridgestone, Goodyear, Continental, Yokohama, Toyo, plus budget and import brands. Recap brands: Bandag (Bridgestone), Michelin Retread Technologies, Goodyear UniCircle.

## More detail

![Read the tread: steer and trailer have ribs, drive has blocks](fs-tread.png)

Tires are usually a fleet's biggest maintenance cost after fuel, and one of the most common road service calls (blowouts, flats) and DOT violations.

## Reading a tire size: 295/75R22.5

- **295** = width in millimeters.
- **75** = sidewall height as a percentage of the width (the aspect ratio).
- **R** = radial construction (all modern truck tires).
- **22.5** = the wheel (rim) diameter in inches.

The common truck sizes are **295/75R22.5**, **11R22.5**, **11R24.5** and **285/75R24.5**. A tire and wheel must match: a 22.5 tire only fits a 22.5 wheel. **Super singles** (wide base tires like 445/50R22.5) replace a pair of duals with one wide tire to save weight and fuel.

**Tire types by position.** A tire is designed for where it goes. **Steer** tires have ribs running around the tire for straight tracking. **Drive** tires have deep blocky lugs for traction (the DR in "Vantage Max DR" from the invoice earlier). **Trailer** tires are built for low rolling resistance and scrubbing in turns. Most tires also carry a load range, like **LR G** or **LR H** (14 or 16 ply rated), which is how much weight they can carry.

**Identifying a tire from a photo.** Check the tread first (drawing above), then the background. A cab door, fuel tank or steps behind the tire means steer or drive. The trailer's metal box, landing gear or the back doors means trailer. A single tire with a big domed cap in the center is a steer. Two tires side by side right under the fifth wheel are drives. The sidewall usually settles it: the model name often ends in a position code (SA, DR, TR), and it also shows the size and the DOT date code.

**The sidewall DOT code.** The last four digits are the week and year it was made: **2224** = week 22 of 2024. Tires older than about 5 to 6 years are questionable even with good tread.

**Tread depth** is measured in 32nds of an inch with a gauge.

|  | New tire | Legal minimum (DOT) |
|---|---|---|
| Steer | about 18/32" to 20/32" | 4/32" |
| Drive | about 26/32" to 32/32" | 2/32" |
| Trailer | about 12/32" to 16/32" | 2/32" |

**Air pressure.** Truck tires usually run around **100 to 110 psi**, checked cold. Underinflated tires overheat and blow out, and they are the main cause of tire failure. Duals should be at the same pressure and matched in size, or one tire does all the work.

## New, used, retread

- **New (virgin):** straight from the maker. Premium brands (Michelin, Bridgestone, Goodyear, Continental) vs budget brands (lots of names; cheaper, wear faster).
- **Used:** graded **A, B, C** by the seller with no official standard. Grade A = lots of tread, even wear, no repairs. A **take off** is a nearly new tire pulled from a new truck.
- **Retread (recap):** a used casing with new tread bonded on. Very common on drive and trailer positions because it is much cheaper; many fleets don't put them on steer. The shreds of rubber you see on highways are often from underinflated retreads.
- **Casing:** the body of a used tire, which has value for retreading.

**Tire problems you'll see on invoices:** flats (a nail), blowouts, irregular wear (from alignment or pressure), sidewall cuts, **road service tire call** (a mobile truck comes to change it on the roadside), mount and balance charges, valve stems, and "dual matching."

**Wheels.** Steel or aluminum (aluminum is lighter and shinier, and more expensive). They're held on with 10 **lug nuts** per wheel, tightened to a set torque. Loose lug nuts leave rust streaks, and a wheel coming off is one of the deadliest failures there is. **Hubs** hold the wheel bearings and **wheel seals** keep oil in.
